<p><em><span style="font-weight: 400;">This article is general information, not medical advice. <p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The honest answer to "how can I improve my hearing naturally?" has two parts.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Damage to the tiny sensory hair cells in the inner ear is permanent, and no food, herb or exercise has been shown to bring it back.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But a lot of hearing trouble comes from things that can be treated, such as earwax buildup or an ear infection, and almost all noise damage can be prevented.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">So the best natural approach is to protect the hearing you have, rule out fixable causes with a hearing test, and be skeptical of products that promise to restore what is lost.</span></p>
<h2><strong>What "Natural" Can and Cannot Do for Your Hearing</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Hearing works when sound waves reach the inner ear and hair cells turn them into signals for the brain.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Loud noise, aging, some medicines, illness and genetics can damage those cells.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">According to the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD), hair cells in humans cannot be fixed or replaced, so noise-related hearing loss is permanent.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That does not mean you are stuck.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Some causes of muffled hearing are temporary and treatable, including wax blockage, fluid behind the eardrum and infections.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Other changes can be slowed by protecting your ears and managing conditions like high blood pressure and diabetes.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">"Natural" works best as prevention and smart daily habits, not as a cure.</span></p>

<h2><strong>Protect Your Ears From Loud Noise</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Noise is the biggest preventable threat to hearing.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The NIDCD explains in its piece on how</span><a href="https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/news/2021/noise-induced-hearing-loss-preventable"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">noise-induced hearing loss is preventable</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> that, according to the CDC, about 40 million US adults ages 20 to 69 have hearing loss from noise, and more than half of them report no loud noise at work.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Common threats include sirens, lawn equipment and loud music, live or through earbuds.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sounds at or below about 70 decibels are not likely to cause hearing loss, while noises of 85 decibels and above can. Practical steps include:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Turn headphone volume down, and use the 60/60 rule: no more than about 60% volume for about 60 minutes at a time.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Wear earplugs or earmuffs at concerts, in loud workplaces and when using power tools, mowers or leaf blowers.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Move away from loud sources, and give your ears quiet breaks afterward.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Choose noise-canceling headphones so you do not crank the volume to drown out background noise.</span></li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>Get a Hearing Test and Rule Out Fixable Causes</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If you suspect your hearing is slipping, the most reliable first step is a hearing test.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The University of California San Francisco's EARS resource on</span><a href="https://ears.ucsf.edu/en/understanding/noise-induced-hearing-loss"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">noise-induced hearing loss</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> notes that many people first notice trouble understanding speech in background noise or persistent ringing, and that a hearing test is the most reliable way to find out.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A clinician can check for causes that are treatable, such as:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Earwax blocking the ear canal</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Fluid or infection in the middle ear</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A perforated eardrum</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Side effects from medicines</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Do not push cotton swabs, hairpins or other objects into your ear canal.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">They can pack wax deeper or injure the eardrum.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If wax is the problem, a clinician can remove it safely.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Lifestyle Habits That Support Ear Health</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Several everyday habits support the blood flow and nerves your ears depend on, though none can undo hair-cell damage:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Eat a balanced diet.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Some research links patterns like the Mediterranean and DASH diets with a lower risk of hearing loss, although this evidence is observational and a food-based diet is not a hearing treatment.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Stay active.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Regular walking and exercise help circulation.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Manage blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Heart and blood vessel health affects the inner ear.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Do not smoke,</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and limit alcohol.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Sleep well and manage stress,</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> since stress can make ringing feel louder.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Keep ears dry after swimming</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> to lower the risk of outer ear infections.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If you take medicines that can affect hearing, such as certain antibiotics, chemotherapy drugs or high-dose aspirin, ask your prescriber whether hearing checks are advised.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Never stop a prescribed medicine on your own.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Do Supplements and Herbs Help Hearing?</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This is where marketing runs far ahead of evidence.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A 2016 survey in the American Journal of Audiology,</span><a href="https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2016_aja-16-0021"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">on dietary supplements for tinnitus</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, found that some supplements were reported to help sleep for some people, but concluded that they were generally not effective and that many produced side effects.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The most used were Ginkgo biloba, lipoflavonoids, magnesium, melatonin, vitamin B12 and zinc.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Ginkgo is a good example.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A Cochrane review, as summarized by nutrition educators, found little to no effect on tinnitus scores compared with placebo.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Correcting a true deficiency is different: a low vitamin B12 or zinc level can contribute to symptoms in some people, which is why a doctor's blood test beats guessing.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Taking extra of a nutrient you do not lack has not been shown to improve hearing.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Supplements can also interact with medicines.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Ginkgo, for example, can affect bleeding risk. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before trying any.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Coping With Ringing in the Ears</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Ringing, buzzing or hissing, called tinnitus, affects many adults and sometimes goes along with hearing loss.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Some natural approaches can ease how much it bothers you, even if they do not remove the sound:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Use background sound, such as a fan, soft music or a sound machine, especially at night.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Try relaxation, mindfulness or breathing exercises.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Limit caffeine and alcohol if they seem to make the sound worse.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Ask about cognitive behavioral therapy and sound therapy, which have more support than pills.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">See a doctor if ringing affects only one ear, pulses with your heartbeat, comes with dizziness or hearing loss, or follows a head injury.</span></p>
